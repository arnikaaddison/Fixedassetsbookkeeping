IRS Notices

I Received a Letter From the IRS—What Should I Do Next?

You open the mailbox and see an envelope from the Internal Revenue Service. Immediately, your mind starts racing. Do I owe money? Am I being audited? Did I do something wrong?

First, understand this: receiving an IRS notice does not automatically mean you're being audited or that you're in serious trouble. The IRS sends notices for many different reasons. The important thing is to determine what the IRS is asking for, what tax year is involved, whether there is a deadline, and whether the information in the notice is correct.

And whatever you do: don't ignore the letter.

Why Does the IRS Send Notices?

An IRS notice can be triggered by many different situations — a balance due, a return that wasn't filed, information that doesn't match your return, a proposed change, a request for more information, an adjusted refund, an identity verification request, or collection activity. Two envelopes can both say "Internal Revenue Service" while requiring completely different responses. Read the notice before reacting to the notice.

Step 1: Identify the Notice Number

Most IRS notices and letters contain an identifying number, such as CP14, CP2000, CP501, CP503, or CP504. That number matters because different notices have different purposes. Before deciding what to do, identify exactly what you received.

Step 2: Check the Tax Year

Determine which tax period the notice concerns. Don't automatically assume it's about the tax return you filed most recently — an IRS notice could relate to a return from several years ago. This becomes particularly important if you have multiple years of tax issues.

Step 3: Find the Response Deadline

This may be the most important part of the entire letter. Look for language such as "Respond by," "Pay by," or "You have 30 days." Do not put the letter in a drawer and plan to deal with it later. Missing a deadline can limit your options or allow the IRS process to move forward. Put the deadline on your calendar immediately.

Step 4: Determine What the IRS Says Happened

Don't focus only on the amount shown in bold. Read the explanation. You need to understand why the IRS believes you owe money or why they're requesting additional information.

Step 5: Compare the Notice With Your Records

An IRS notice isn't automatically correct simply because it came from the IRS. But it shouldn't automatically be assumed to be wrong either. Compare the notice with your own records — your tax return, W-2s, 1099s, bank statements, proof of payments, business records, and any prior IRS correspondence or transcripts.

What If the IRS Says You Forgot to Report Income?

Before agreeing to a proposed adjustment, determine whether the income is actually yours, whether it was already reported somewhere else on the return, whether the amount reported was correct, and whether related expenses were properly considered. First determine whether the proposed change is accurate before immediately paying it.

What If You Actually Owe the Money?

If you review the notice and determine the balance is legitimate, the next question becomes what to do if you can't pay it all at once. Don't assume that being unable to pay the entire balance means you should avoid responding — depending on your circumstances and eligibility, there may be collection or payment options to explore.

What If You Haven't Filed All Your Tax Returns?

This can change the conversation. Before focusing only on the balance shown on the newest letter, it may be necessary to determine which returns are missing, which years have balances, and whether the IRS has already taken action on any unfiled years. This is where IRS transcripts can become extremely useful.

Should You Call the IRS Yourself?

You can contact the IRS regarding your own tax account, but prepare before making the call. Have the notice and your tax return in front of you, write down your questions, and keep notes of the date you called, who you spoke with, what was discussed, and any deadline or next step you were given. Don't rely on memory — create a record of the conversation.

Don't Send Random Documents to the IRS

If the IRS asks you to substantiate a specific item, your response should address that issue. Sending a large stack of unrelated documents can make your response harder to understand. Your documentation should tell a clear story: here's what was questioned, here's the response, here's the supporting documentation.

Don't Automatically Amend Your Tax Return

Receiving an IRS notice doesn't automatically mean you need to file an amended return. Sometimes a written response is appropriate. Sometimes documentation is needed. Sometimes no change to the original return is necessary. Before amending, understand what the notice is actually asking you to do.

The Biggest Mistake: Ignoring the Notice

IRS letters can be intimidating — that's exactly why some people don't open them. One unopened letter becomes two, then three, and what started as a manageable issue may become more complicated. Opening the envelope doesn't create the problem. It gives you information about the problem.

What Should You Do With Your IRS Notice Right Now?

1. Open the letter

Don't avoid it.

2. Read every page

Important information may appear beyond the first page.

3. Identify the notice number

Look for a CP or letter number.

4. Identify the tax year

Determine exactly which period is involved.

5. Locate the deadline

Write it down immediately.

6. Determine what the IRS wants

Payment? Documentation? A response? Verification?

7. Gather the related tax return and records

Don't respond before understanding what was originally filed.

8. Get help if you don't understand the notice

Especially when the notice involves a significant balance, proposed assessment, examination, collection activity, multiple tax years, or a deadline you don't understand.

You Don't Need to Guess What the IRS Wants

IRS notices are written for a tax administration process—not necessarily for easy reading. You may know the IRS wants something without understanding exactly what you're supposed to provide. That's where a proper notice review can help.
