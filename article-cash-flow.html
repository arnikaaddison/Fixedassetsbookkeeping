Business Finances

My Business Is Making Money—So Where Is All the Money Going?

Your business made $15,000 this month. You paid your expenses, checked your bank account, and somehow there's only $2,300 left. So you ask the question almost every business owner has asked at some point: Where did all my money go?

Here's the first thing you need to understand: Revenue, profit, and cash are not the same thing.

A business can generate strong sales and still struggle to pay its bills. It can even show a profit on its Profit & Loss Statement while the owner feels like there is never enough cash in the bank. Understanding the difference can completely change how you manage your business.

Revenue Is Not What You Get to Keep

Revenue is generally the money your business earns from selling its products or services before expenses are deducted. Suppose your business generates $20,000 in monthly revenue. That sounds great.

But then the business pays $4,000 in payroll or contractors, $2,500 in rent, $1,200 in advertising, $900 in software, $1,500 in supplies, and $1,000 in insurance and other expenses. You've already spent $11,100 — and we haven't considered every possible use of your cash.

This is why saying "my business made $20,000 this month" doesn't necessarily mean "I made $20,000 personally." Those are two very different statements.

What Is Profit?

Profit is generally what's left after business expenses are subtracted from business revenue. In a simplified example: Revenue $20,000, Business Expenses $14,000, Net Profit $6,000.

That $6,000 gives you important information about how the business performed. But here's where things get interesting — you can have a $6,000 profit and still wonder why you don't have $6,000 sitting in your checking account. That's because profit and cash flow measure different things.

What Is Cash Flow?

Cash flow looks at money moving into and out of your business. Money comes in. Money goes out. And timing matters. Your business may have expenses or cash outflows that don't appear on the Profit & Loss Statement in the way you expect. You may also have earned revenue that hasn't actually been collected yet.

Reason #1: You're Taking Money Out of the Business

Business owners need money to live. You may transfer $500 here, $1,000 there, pay a personal expense, or take regular owner withdrawals. Those withdrawals can reduce the cash sitting in the business bank account.

Depending on your business structure and circumstances, an owner's draw or distribution isn't necessarily reported as an operating expense on your Profit & Loss Statement. So your P&L might show Net Profit of $8,000, but if you've taken $5,000 out of the business, you won't necessarily see $8,000 sitting in your bank account.

Reason #2: You're Paying Down Debt

Suppose your business has a loan with a $1,200 monthly payment. That payment reduces the cash in your bank account. However, accounting for loan payments isn't always as simple as recording the entire $1,200 as an expense — part of the payment may reduce the loan balance, while another part may represent interest.

Cash leaving the business doesn't always equal an expense on your P&L.

Reason #3: Customers Haven't Paid You Yet

This can be a major issue for service businesses. You may complete $10,000 worth of work and send invoices to your customers. Your business generated the sales. But if your customers haven't paid those invoices yet, you may not have the cash.

You need to know who owes you money, how much they owe, and how long the invoice has been outstanding. A business can look successful on paper and still struggle with cash because too much money is sitting in unpaid invoices.

Reason #4: You're Spending Without a Plan

Small expenses are sneaky. $29.99 here, $79 there, another $150 subscription. A few meals. Advertising. Software. Apps. Memberships. Contractors. Supplies. Individually, the amounts may not feel significant. Collectively, they can consume thousands of dollars every month.

This is why simply looking at your bank balance isn't enough. Your Profit & Loss Statement should help you see where your money is actually going.

Reason #5: You're Not Setting Aside Money for Taxes

For many business owners—especially self-employed individuals—taxes aren't automatically withheld from every payment received. That can create a dangerous illusion: you receive $5,000 from customers and see $5,000 in the bank, but that doesn't necessarily mean the entire $5,000 is available to spend.

Depending on your circumstances, some of that money may eventually be needed for federal taxes, state taxes, estimated tax payments, payroll taxes, or other obligations. Tax planning should be part of your financial routine—not a surprise once a year.

Reason #6: You're Growing Too Fast

Yes, growth can create cash problems. Imagine your business suddenly doubles its sales. Great news, right? Usually. But now you may need more employees, more inventory, more supplies, more equipment, additional software, a larger space, and more advertising.

You may have to spend money before the additional revenue reaches your bank account. That's why a growing business can still experience cash-flow pressure. Growth needs to be financially planned.

The Three Numbers Every Business Owner Should Understand

You don't need to become an accountant. But there are three numbers you should be able to discuss confidently:

1. Revenue

How much money is your business generating?

2. Profit

After your business expenses, how much is the business actually earning?

3. Cash

How much money does the business currently have available?

These numbers are connected—but they aren't interchangeable. Understanding the difference helps you make much better decisions.

Stop Running Your Business From Your Bank Balance

You log into your bank account. You see $14,000. And immediately you think, "I have $14,000 to spend." Not necessarily. Some of that money may already have a job — payroll coming up, rent due, vendor bills, credit-card payments, tax obligations, loan payments, upcoming operating expenses.

Your bank balance tells you how much cash is sitting there. It doesn't tell you how much of that cash is truly available for you to spend.

Start Giving Your Money a Job

Instead of waiting until the end of the month to see what's left, start planning where the money needs to go — operating expenses, payroll, taxes, debt, owner compensation, emergency reserves, future growth. Your exact structure will depend on your business, but the principle is simple: don't let every dollar that enters the account become available for random spending.

Review Your Numbers Every Month

At minimum, business owners should regularly review their Profit & Loss Statement, Balance Sheet, Accounts Receivable, and Bank and Credit-Card Reconciliations. Your reports shouldn't just exist because your accountant needs them. You should be using them to run your business.

Your Business Can Make Money and Still Be Broke

That sounds contradictory, but it happens every day. Strong sales don't automatically create strong finances. You need revenue, profitability, cash-flow management, accurate bookkeeping, and financial visibility.
